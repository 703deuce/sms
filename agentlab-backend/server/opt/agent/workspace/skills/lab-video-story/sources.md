# Retention sources (labels used in reference.md)
Status key (same as `craft-standards` / `sources.md`):
- **P**: primary source (the person, platform, or paper), fetched and read.
- **E**: editor, strategist or educator speaking in their own video, podcast or book interview; fetched via a transcript or a full interview page.
- **V**: vendor or tool write-up. Used only when it clearly reports a named practitioner, and marked as such.
- **S**: secondary summary of a primary (a careful recap, an index of a podcast, a leaked-memo mirror). Claims taken only where they match or quote the primary.

Raw copies: MrBeast memo text is in `raw/retention/mrbeast-memo.txt` (PDF at `raw/retention/mrbeast-memo.pdf`). Other pages were read live.

| MB-MEMO | P | https://sambharia.com/assets/mrbeast-production-memo.pdf (discussed at https://simonwillison.net/2024/Sep/15/how-to-succeed-in-mrbeast-production/) | Leaked MrBeast production onboarding memo. Goal is the best *YouTube* video, not the best-produced one. Metrics: CTR, AVD, AVP. Title/thumbnail set expectations; a mismatch (yellow "world's largest" castle vs a smaller red one in a field) makes people feel lied to and leave. First minute has the most loss on almost every YouTube video; on one example they lost 21 million of 60 million clickers in the first minute and called that above average for them. Front-load interest, light it, pre-plan the first lines, match the click. Minutes 1–3: hype to execution, "crazy progression" (cover several days of a survival story in the first 3 minutes), and a 3-minute re-engagement (a story-fit spectacle "only MrBeast can do"; example: Karl watching Josh in the prison video). Minutes 3–6: simple, exciting, lots of quick scene changes; if they watch the first half they will likely finish; another re-engagement around 6 minutes. Back half: do not signal the end except to hype the payoff; people may be in a "lull." Average of last 100 videos: 818 seconds, 13 min 37 s. "Wow factor" example: house flown in on a crane at 30 s in the 100-days-in-a-circle video. Two same-length videos, first-day data: the one people watched 1 min 38 s longer went to 120 M views vs 45 M. Working formula: hook, invest them in a story, no dull moments, satisfying payoff, abrupt ending. Formats: last-to-leave (result withheld), stair-stepping (the firework video: $1, $10, $50, $375, $1,000, $10,000, then later $40,000, $100,000, world record), chase formats. Old mid-roll brand deals left a "crater" on the retention graph; integrated, entertaining brand deals left a smaller dip. "Brand deals are content." Rules: no dull moments; you can't fake intensity; endings must always be abrupt to protect retention; never put him in a situation where he has to lie. |
| PADDY-ISLAND | E | https://pickscribe.com/v/L4rXidEr248/ (video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L4rXidEr248) | Paddy Galloway's breakdown of MrBeast's 100-million-subscriber island video, read from a full transcript. Opening 20 s: a visual change every 1.1 s (his measurement of *this* video, not a universal law). Intro formula he reads across Beast videos: open fast, establish scale, transition to the first challenge. First challenge starts at 17 s. Establishing shots used to wow with production scale (zoom out to the island; 100 contestants in a line). Typical YouTube energy graph spikes at the open, sags, rises at the end; this video raises tension every few minutes with a new challenge. Videos finish on a high point (dramatic or wholesome) that leaves the lasting impression. Cutting a lot and talking fast is not enough. | HOYOS-CS | E | https://creatorscience.com/podcast/jenny-hoyos/ | Jenny Hoyos on the Creator Science podcast (10 Oct 2023), full transcript. A good Short: a hook that could work as a long-form title/thumbnail, very visual, understandable without sound, simple enough to explain to a 5-year-old, plus a story people will rewatch. "Every second counts." "Whatever you say you're gonna do, you end it right after you do it." "If you lose one second, that's already 97%." Bare-minimum Shorts retention, in her opinion, is 90%+ *because of rewatches*. Mechanism: "the circle is constantly closing"; showing "3 steps" or "3 things" sets a clear expectation of the end. Cold-open the payoff (showing the gift) then earn it and close the loop. Personal stakes and irony (broken kitchen, so she has to cook to raise the repair money). Later hot take: retention is not everything. She has seen friends' 40 s Shorts with 100%+ retention and ~100 k views fail to break out, and one of her own Shorts at ~70% retention on the first 100 k views still reach ~10 M, carried by returning viewers. One Short had a 20% share-to-view ratio and ~92% "growth rate" (her phrasing). Her hunch: satisfaction and shares, not the graph alone. |
| HOYOS-MFM | E | https://www.mfmindex.com/episodes/580 | Transcript index of My First Million ep. 580, Hoyos speaking. Hook with power words (free, $0, stole, or another strong word) and with action, not "This is…," which she scrolls past. Foreshadow the end in the hook (conflict + what will be at the end). Implied foreshadowing ("who has the best dollar menu?") is clean; a *mechanism* is stronger ("What does $10 get you in Miami?" plus a visible running budget). If they cannot feel progress toward the end, they leave even if you promised something. Team target is generally at least 90%, usually 95%+. Twists and tight tutorials/listicles drive rewatches. Pay off the loop; do not skip the payoff. |
| HOYOS-YT | E | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WlRt8Ov4kBk | Hoyos, "How to Get Over 100% Retention on YouTube Shorts." If a Short is under 30 s, her observation is that it usually needs over 100% retention (rewatches) to take off. Structure she uses: a very short hook, then foreshadow the end, then a transition that is "a pacing break without the pacing actually breaking." She rejects "let's get started" after a dense hook (too much setup; recency) and replaces it with a line that already moves the story ("so I cooked illegally"). |

| EVES-5 | P | https://derraleves.com/5-things-that-youtube-wants-all-creators-to-do/ | Derral Eves, on his own site: hook within 5–7 seconds with something engaging or pleasing to the eye, then extend the hook to 20–30 seconds. |
| EVES-PI | E | https://www.productiveinsights.com/podcast/derral-eves-youtube-tips | Eves interview. After the click, "their tolerance level is about 15 seconds" to check that this is what they clicked on. Then "about a minute, a minute to prove yourself," and "about a minute and a half to fulfill." He has seen openings lose "50, sometimes 60% of the people" when the story is not hooked and the title/thumbnail is not validated. Favours a "progressional setup" that climbs to a climax. Mentions a teaser-after-the-credits instinct (people waiting through film credits for the next-story tease) as a reason a forward hook at the end can work. |
| EVES-NS | E | https://podcast.nealschaffer.com/episode/the-youtube-formula-the-definitive-guide-to-youtube-marketing-derral-eves-interview | Eves: focus on the first minute; "cutting everything out no branding no nothing," get right to what the title and thumbnail promised. "Kill your babies": even a wonderful moment comes out if it "distracts from that viewing pattern, that viewing journey." |
| HHS-FORBES | E | https://www.forbes.com/sites/jonyoushaei/2023/03/10/how-hayden-hillier-smith-redefined-what-it-means-to-be-an-editor-on-youtube/ | Hayden Hillier-Smith (Logan Paul's editor, later The Editing Podcast) in a Forbes interview with Jon Youshaei. Every stretch of an edit should hit emotion, story and rhythm; two of three is not enough. Leitmotifs and added SFX heighten a moment. Don't open with five shots of exposition; also don't withhold all context. "The story is revealing that, and making a response" (cut to the Wrestlemania jump when Logan mentions it). Slow cuts build tension, fast cuts are the payoff; sometimes you "don't quite have enough time to see the image," which adds curiosity; a slow zoom with no story payoff feels empty. YouTube is not a theatre: an establishing shot that would work in a cinema gets "what the (redacted) is this?" on YouTube. Every edit should convince the viewer to stay. |
| SCHOON | E | https://nofilmschool.com/slap-the-audience-schoonmaker | Thelma Schoonmaker, quoted via a 2011 DP/30 interview as reported by No Film School (Jan 2026 recap). "A lot of people think editing should be invisible, but we've never felt that way." "We always like to slap the audience in the face if it's required." Scorsese encourages holding a shot longer than is comfortable, then cutting when it is justified. She rejects a "cut every three seconds" rule: "Each shot has its own life. You have to learn how to make it live." |
| SCHOON-SIL | E | https://www.provideocoalition.com/art-of-the-cut-thelma-schoonmaker/ | Schoonmaker to Steve Hullfish, Art of the Cut, on *Silence*. | HARRIS-JY | S / E | Summary: https://podwise.ai/episodes/2768274 — interview: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fa1_VWuIElE | Johnny Harris interviewed by Jon Youshaei, "The Rise of Johnny Harris, Explained" (Nov 2024). Claims used were taken from the Podwise interview summary and Q&A, which attributes them to Harris, and should be treated as a careful secondary of that interview. Writing aims at roughly a fifth-to-eighth-grade reading level. The team uses a two-column script (narration / detailed visual direction, including music and animation). |

Used together with HARRIS-JY, not on its own. |
| PREECE | E | https://pickscribe.com/v/LYZ2-ge9xqs (video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LYZ2-ge9xqs) | Greg Preece, Ali Abdaal's scriptwriter, on a retention-graph series (full transcript). Pattern he names "the gradual realization": nothing obviously breaks, the edit can still look lively, but viewers slowly decide the video has already given them its big payoff. Example: a "non-horror fan reviews [horror game]" video in which, after about a minute, the host says the game "didn't scare me too much," then reviews the game — the packaged question is already answered, and people slide off. |
| LOEW | P | https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/PsychofCuriosity.pdf | George Loewenstein, "The Psychology of Curiosity: A Review and Reinterpretation," *Psychological Bulletin* 116(1), 75–98 (1994). Curiosity as "cognitively induced deprivation that arises from the perception of a gap in knowledge or understanding." He also notes curiosity's intensity, transience, and "tendency to disappoint when satisfied." A less obvious implication, which he tests and discusses: curiosity rises with knowledge in a domain, because you begin to frame the situation around what you *don't* know (his 47-vs-3 state-capitals illustration). |
| PEAK | P | https://doi.org/10.1037//0022-3514.65.1.45 (PDF: https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/fredr-kahneman-jpsp1993.pdf) | Fredrickson & Kahneman, "Duration neglect in retrospective evaluations of affective episodes," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 65(1), 45–55 (1993). Retrospective evaluations are largely insensitive to duration and instead resemble a weighted average of salient affect snapshots, especially the most intense moment and the ending — the peak-end rule. |
| SOK | P | https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev.ph.25.030163.002553 | E. N. Sokolov, "Higher Nervous Functions: The Orienting Reflex," *Annual Review of Physiology* 25 (1963). A novel stimulus triggers the orienting reflex; repeated presentations build a neuronal model and the response habituates; a changed stimulus restores the mismatch and re-orients attention. Used as the psychological basis for a *story-motivated* pattern interrupt, not as a "cut every n seconds" law. |
| SWELL | P | https://doi.org/10.1016/0364-0213(88)90023-7 | John Sweller, "Cognitive Load During Problem Solving: Effects on Learning," *Cognitive Science* 12 (1988). Working memory is limited; overloading it with simultaneous novel demands leaves little capacity for understanding. Used here as the reason to present one new idea (and one new graphic) at a time, not as a classroom-instruction manual. |
| GAZE | P | https://doi.org/10.1080/13506280444000157 | Senju & Hasegawa, "Direct gaze captures visuospatial attention," *Visual Cognition* 12(1), 85–94 (2005). A face with direct gaze delays detection of a later peripheral target — direct eye contact holds attention. |
| FACES | P | PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16615327/ | Bindemann, Burton, Hooge, Jenkins & de Haan, "Faces retain attention," *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* (2005). Upright faces delay disengagement of attention more than objects, inverted faces or names. |
| ZEIG-META | P | https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05000-w | 2025 meta-analysis of the Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina effects (38 studies). No overall memory advantage for interrupted over completed tasks (interrupted-to-completed recall averaged 0.99). Included so we *don't* treat the popular "unfinished tasks are remembered better" line as settled science. Open loops in this file are grounded in Loewenstein and in editor practice instead. |
