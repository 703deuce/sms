# Story and research: reference

## Beat sheet for a 30-60 s documentary (example shape, any topic)
| # | Beat | Share | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cold fact / hook | ~10% | Outcome or paradox, flat, concrete (a number, a date, a verdict) |
| 2 | Stakes / premise | ~10-15% | Who, where, why it mattered to someone |
| 3 | Turning point | ~25% | The decision, event or discovery that drove it |
| 4 | Consequence / escalation | ~25% | What followed, with sourced numbers |
| 5 | Reversal or open question | ~15% | Reframes the hook or names what is still unknown |
| 6 | Closing line | ~10% | One sentence that answers the hook |

Hook patterns that work: stated outcome with the cause withheld ("The flight lasted twelve seconds. It changed
everything."), a contradiction ("The richest man in town died owing money."), a precise number. Weak: "Here
are three facts", "You won't believe".

## Fact-check checklist
- Every name spelled from the source; every date checked against two sources; every number traced to a named
  source. Keep `log/facts.md` as an annotated script (claim -> URL -> quote).
- Separate what a court/official body found from what a party alleged. Use "prosecutors say", "police said",
  "according to court records".
- Sensitive subjects (crime, minors, victims): no speculation, no invented recreations presented as real,
  label recreations, prefer public records and on-record statements.

## 1.15 Hooks and retention: what the platforms say
- **TikTok:**
  - Introduce your proposition in the **first 3 s (90 f)** and prioritise the hook in the first **6 s** [TT-CBP]. The Creator Academy says to hook within the first 5 s [TT-CA1].
  - Hook techniques it lists include teasing the end result and starting at the climax, then backtracking [TT-CA1]. Use drop-off analytics to find where viewers leave [TT-CA2].
  - Structure: Freytag's pyramid. "Motivation, obstacle, punchline." One key message at a time [TT-CA1].
- **YouTube Shorts.** Studio's **"Stayed to watch"** is the share of viewers who watch past a Short's opening seconds. "Engaged views" exclude loops [YT-AN][YT-SH]. The Shorts upload flow offers feedback on a video's "hook, pacing, and more" [YT-SH].
- **Instagram Reels.** Instagram predicts how likely you are to "watch a reel all the way through", reshare it and like it [IG-RANK]. Reels up to **3 min** are eligible for recommendation [IG-3MIN].
- **Google ABCD** [ABCD]:
  - Use tight framing on people or product at the open, with **more than two shots in the first 5 s (150 f)**.
  - If the video has people, open on them, ideally speaking to the viewer.
  - Introduce the brand within 5 s.

# Viewer psychology and retention: depth (why people watch and keep watching)

General structural principles, not platform rules; never a look or a template. Must/Should/Freedom as in the craft
standards. The script-stage checklist is in `SKILL.md`; the edit-stage checklist below is applied in review on the
change-point stills and contact sheet before render.

## 1.2 Deliver on the promise, immediately

**Why.** People click or stop scrolling because the packaging (title, thumbnail, first frame, first line) opened a gap between what they know and what they want to know. If the opening does not confirm that this video will close that gap, they leave. That is information-gap theory in practice [LOEW], and it is also what YouTube, Derral Eves and the MrBeast memo all say in plainer words.

- Curiosity, in Loewenstein's account, is "cognitively induced deprivation that arises from the perception of a gap in knowledge." It is sharp, easily satisfied, and often a little disappointing once it is filled — which is why the *journey* to the answer has to be satisfying, not only the answer itself [LOEW]. People also grow more curious as they gain a little knowledge: a person who knows 47 state capitals feels the three they are missing; a person who knows three does not [LOEW]. So a hook should make the viewer feel they already know *enough* to care, and not enough to leave.
- YouTube: the opening seconds are "when viewers decide to stay or move on." The intro must deliver on the thumbnail and title, stay concise, and "get straight to providing that value," then keep people there by building "anticipation and curiosity" [YT-REC][YT-GROW].
- Derral Eves's working tolerances (his rules of thumb, from interviews, not platform law): hook in **5–7 seconds** with something engaging or pleasing to the eye, then extend that hook through **20–30 seconds** [EVES-5]. A viewer's first check is about **15 seconds**: "is this what I actually clicked on?" Then they give you roughly **a minute to a minute and a half** to prove their time will not be wasted. He has seen openings lose **50%, sometimes 60%** of the audience when that validation never comes [EVES-PI].
- MrBeast memo: the title and thumbnail set expectations. If they clicked "World's Largest Bouncy Castle" and a yellow castle beside tall buildings, and you give them a smaller red castle in an empty field, "you'd feel like you were lied to and click off." Everyone on the job must know the title and thumbnail so the first minute can meet it [MB-MEMO]. In one example they still lost **21 million of 60 million** clickers in the first minute and called that *above average* for them — which is why they "freak out" about that minute: front-load interesting material, light it well, pre-plan the first lines, and match the click [MB-MEMO].
- Eves's other operational rule: "kill your babies." A beautiful shot or joke that interrupts the viewing journey comes out, even if it is the most amazing thing in the bin [EVES-NS].

**Must:** the opening confirms the promise the packaging (or, on a feed with no title, the first frame and first line) just made. **Must:** do not bait a claim the film will not pay off. MrBeast's own line is "Don't ever put me in a situation where I have to lie, because I won't and it will screw the video" [MB-MEMO].

## 1.3 The first frame, and the first 1–3 seconds

**Why.** In a feed, the first frame *is* the thumbnail. The eye orients to novelty, faces, contrast and motion before a sentence has finished. If the first frame is a logo, a black card, or a vague wide shot, the swipe has already started.

- Jenny Hoyos, on Shorts: a hook has to work as if it were a title and thumbnail on a long-form video. "The hook needs to be very visual. You need to understand it without even listening to it, and it needs to be so simple… explain to a 5 year old" [HOYOS-CS]. Start with a power word or an action, not a label. "This is…" tells the viewer nothing and she scrolls; "Free chicken sandwiches all weekend" or "I'm building a secret room" already starts the story [HOYOS-MFM].
- On a short, every second is a large share of the runtime. Hoyos: "If you lose one second, that's already 97%" (on a roughly 30-second Short, one lost second is about 3 percentage points of average percentage viewed) [HOYOS-CS]. For Shorts **under 30 seconds**, her observation is that they usually need **over 100%** average percentage viewed (rewatches) to take off [HOYOS-YT].
- Hayden Hillier-Smith (Logan Paul's long-time editor): a cinema audience has paid to sit through an establishing shot. A YouTube viewer has not. "On YouTube, you click on the video — establishing shot — 'What the (redacted) is this?'" Five shots of exposition at the top will bore them; starting with no context at all will confuse them. The opening has to *reveal* the story and make a response [HHS-FORBES].
- Paddy Galloway's frame-by-frame of MrBeast's 100-million-subscriber island video (one video, measured by him, not a universal cadence): the opening **20 seconds** changed visually every **1.1 seconds**; the first challenge started at **17 seconds**; and the intro formula he reads across Beast videos is "open fast, establish scale, and transition to the first challenge" [PADDY-ISLAND]. Scale is a hook of its own: zoom out from Jimmy to the whole island, then show all 100 contestants in a line, so the production feels as big as the promise [PADDY-ISLAND].
- The "wow factor" can sit *inside* those first 30 seconds without being in the title. In "100 days in the circle," they fly the house in on a crane **30 seconds** in, instead of starting on it already built — "who else on YouTube can do that." It did not change the packaging; it hooked people and left an impression [MB-MEMO].
- YouTube Creative Strategy's own hook patterns, useful as a menu not a template: start with a question the brain wants answered; give immediate context so that **within five seconds** the viewer knows what they will get; or hit a cold open and jump into the action [YT-FOUR]. TikTok Creator Academy adds: tease the finished result (and show it again at the end), or set a timer so the viewer knows the commitment [TT-CA2]. Instagram tells creators to hook in the **first 3 seconds** and to watch "View Rate" — the share who are still there after those 3 seconds [IG-HOOK].

**Must:** the first frame is already a hook, readable with the sound off. No logo-first intros, no "hey guys, welcome back," no "today we're going to talk about…" [HOYOS-MFM][EVES-5][MB-MEMO]. **Should:** a verbal hook and a visual hook land together in the first 1–3 s of a short, and in the first 5–15 s of long-form [YT-FOUR][YT-15][EVES-5].

**Freedom:** how the hook is designed — a face, a question, a result-first flash, a cold open, a scale reveal, a concrete object. Invent it for this film.

## 1.4 Open loops, foreshadowing and payoff timing

**Why.** A perceived gap in knowledge pulls attention forward (Loewenstein). Editors call this an open loop. The popular "Zeigarnik effect" (unfinished tasks are remembered better) is the name many strategists use for the same feeling, but the original 1927 memory finding has not held up in a 2025 meta-analysis (interrupted-to-completed recall averaged about 1.0) [ZEIG-META]. Do not cite Zeigarnik as a law. Cite the information gap, and cite what editors actually do with it.

- **Plant a specific unanswered question, then make progress toward it visible.** Hoyos's word for this is foreshadowing: "tell the viewer that there's an Amazon gift card at the end of the video." The cleanest version is implied inside the hook ("Who has the best dollar menu?" already says you will get an answer). A stronger version adds a *mechanism* the viewer can watch close: "What does $10 get you in Miami?" plus an on-screen budget that ticks down, so they can see the circle closing. "If the viewer does not feel like the video is constantly progressing, then they're going to leave because they don't feel like, even though you said that there's something at the end, they don't know where the end is" [HOYOS-MFM][HOYOS-CS].
- **Do not pay off the title in the first minute of a longer video.** Greg Preece (Ali Abdaal's scriptwriter), reviewing real retention graphs, named a pattern he calls the "gradual realization": nothing obviously goes wrong, the edit still looks lively, but the big aha already happened, so people drift off. His example: a video packaged as a non-horror fan reviewing a horror game, in which the host says around **minute one**, "this game didn't scare me too much," and then reviews the game. The people who clicked for that answer have it, and they leave in a slow slide, not a crash [PREECE].
- **Withhold the format's result until the end, and escalate toward it.** MrBeast: in "last to leave," the winner is not revealed until the end, so invested viewers stay if the middle is not boring. In "stair stepping" (the world's-largest-firework video), they light a $1 firework, then $10, $50, $375, $1,000, $10,000, later $40,000, $100,000, then the world record — "as you get deeper in the video the stakes get higher." Chase formats work the same way: "Will they catch me? … Gotta watch until the end" [MB-MEMO].
- **On short-form, foreshadow early, then actually close the loop.** Hoyos will show, in the cold open, the gift being given, then cut back and earn that moment, then "end it right after you do it" [HOYOS-CS]. Skipping the payoff to protect a short-form retention number is a mistake she warns against [HOYOS-MFM].
- **Johnny Harris's documentary version** is a question-and-report cycle built on "visual first storytelling." He looks for a *visual anchor* — a concrete image, object, map, document or data point that immediately shows "something's up" even if you do not know the topic — then a short contextual bridge that explains it, then another anchor. "It's not just putting images next to words; it's about words and imagery dancing together." Context comes after the image, not before [HARRIS-JY][HARRIS-VIS]. Each answer should leave a new question outstanding so the viewer is never holding full information with nothing pending.

**Must:** the script names the main open loop (what the viewer is waiting to see or know), when it is planted, and when it is paid off. **Must:** the promised payoff actually arrives. **Should:** at least one mechanism or escalating stake makes progress visible before the payoff (a countdown, a list of three, a rising cost, a narrowing field of contestants, a question that gets sharper). **Should:** on films longer than about a minute, do not dump the final answer in the opening; tease it, prove you can deliver it, then earn it.

## 1.5 Re-hooks and the usual drop-off points

**Why.** Attention habituates. A story that only peaks at the start and again at the end leaks people in the middle. YouTube's graph almost always falls across the runtime; the work is to stop that fall becoming a cliff, and to put a new reason to stay wherever the curve usually sags [YT-RET].

Typical risk points, from official analytics and from veterans (these are places to *inspect*, not a template for where to put a title card):

| Risk point | What usually failed | Who flags it |
|---|---|---|
| First 1–3 s (short-form) / first 5–15 s (long-form) | First frame or first line was not a hook; packaging mismatch already starting | Hoyos, Eves, YouTube, Instagram [HOYOS-CS][EVES-5][YT-REC][IG-HOOK] |
| First 30 s (YouTube's official "intro") | Opening did not match title/thumbnail, or was a slow setup | YouTube [YT-RET] |
| End of the first minute (long-form) | Hype without execution; still talking about the video instead of showing it | MrBeast memo, Eves [MB-MEMO][EVES-PI] |
| Around 3 minutes, and again around 6 minutes, on a ~10–15 min film | The story has settled; people are ready to leave unless something new and impressive happens | MrBeast memo [MB-MEMO] |
| Mid-film / first-half boundary | They have not yet fallen in love with the story or the people; late "top moments" are still being hoarded | MrBeast memo, YouTube [MB-MEMO][YT-RET] |
| Just before the payoff | The build-up is all delay and no new information, so they skip to the end | Follows from Preece (payoff already felt done) and from Hoyos (progress must stay visible) [PREECE][HOYOS-MFM] |
| A mid-roll, logo, or "and now a word from…" | The film signals a break. People skip, and some leave | MrBeast brand-deal graphs [MB-MEMO] |
| After "thanks for watching" or any line that signals the end | They have permission to leave before the payoff, end screen or loop | MrBeast memo [MB-MEMO] |

Beast production treats a video as four jobs, and everyone on the crew is expected to know which minute they are building. On their channel the average length of the last 100 videos was **13 minutes 37 seconds**, so these marks are for that kind of film, not for a 30-second short [MB-MEMO]:

1. **Minute 0–1.** Most important minute. Meet the click, front-load interest.
2. **Minutes 1–3.** "Transition from hype to execution." Stop telling, start showing. *Crazy progression:* in a 10-minute survival film, do not spend the first 3 minutes on day one the way a "logical filmmaker" would; cover several days so the viewer is already invested and needs to see how much further the person can go. At about **3 minutes**, a *re-engagement*: a highly interesting, story-fit spectacle that "only MrBeast can do" (example: Karl put in charge of watching Josh in the prison-survival video), because this is when people get bored and click off [MB-MEMO].
3. **Minutes 3–6.** The next most important stretch. "The most exciting and interesting content that is also very simple," with "lots of quick scene changes and highly stimulating simple content that reflects the story." The goal is to make them fall in love with the story and the people. "If we can get them to watch the first half of the video there's a very high chance they'll watch to the end." Another re-engagement around **6 minutes**, interesting enough to carry the back half, even if it needs a little more explanation [MB-MEMO].
4. **Back half.** They are often in what the memo calls a "lull": watching without realising they are watching a video. That is not a licence for weaker content — "many videos have been killed because the back half content was ass." Put long explanations here if you must. **Never signal the end** unless you are building hype for the prize or payoff [MB-MEMO].

Paddy, on the same island film: a lot of YouTube videos spike at the open, sag, and only rise again at the end. The island video keeps raising tension every few minutes with a new challenge, so it is hard to skip ahead. He also flags a craft trick that is easy to miss: a contestant restates the rule Jimmy just gave ("the red line… 20 people"), which plants the stake twice without a graphic [PADDY-ISLAND]. Organic, slightly messy moments are left in on purpose so the film still feels like YouTube, not a commercial [PADDY-ISLAND].

On a 30–60-second film, the same architecture *shrinks*: hook and promise in the first 1–3 s, execution immediately, a mid-point re-hook or "but / then" turn, payoff, abrupt end. Hoyos's "but / then" ("But is that enough to beat Chick-fil-A?") is a 30-second version of a 3-minute re-engagement [HOYOS-MKT].

**Must:** the treatment names at least one mid-film re-hook (or, on a short, one mid-point turn) that is new information, a raised stake, or a spectacle that still belongs to the story — not a random cutaway. **Must:** do not signal the end before the payoff. **Should:** if the strongest moment in the bin is sitting in the last third, move a piece of it earlier, as YouTube recommends for "top moments" [YT-RET].

## 1.6 Pacing and pattern interrupts — and when not to use them

**Why.** A repeated, unchanged stimulus stops triggering the orienting response; a change (a new shot, a sound, a face, a contrast shift) dishabituates attention and pulls the eyes back [SOK]. That is the real justification for a "pattern interrupt." It is also why a metronome of cuts becomes its own monotony, and why fatigue sets in when nothing is allowed to land.

This is the principle that is easiest to turn into a bad house style. Do not.

- **Cut count is not pace.** That is already in `craft-standards.md` §1.6, from the same Hollywood shot-length research and editing courses. It stays true here. Paddy, watching people copy Beast: "people often make the mistake of thinking that this is just about having lots of cuts and talking really fast. There's a lot more to it than that" [PADDY-ISLAND]. Thelma Schoonmaker, asked about an editor who cut every three seconds as a rule, rejected it: "Each shot has its own life. You have to learn how to make it live" [SCHOON].
- **Change something when attention would habituate, not on a clock.** The change can be a cut, a camera move, a new graphic, a music shift, a line of text, a new face, or a drop to silence. Paddy measured a **1.1-second** visual-change rate in the *opening 20 seconds* of one MrBeast video [PADDY-ISLAND]. The memo itself only says "lots of quick scene changes" in minutes 3–6, not a seconds interval [MB-MEMO]. There is no licensed "every 3–4 seconds for the whole film" rule in the memo; treat blog claims of that number as unverified.
- **Dynamic rhythm, not constant speed.** Hayden: slow cuts build tension, fast cuts deliver the payoff. Holding a shot so the viewer "doesn't quite have enough time to see the image" can itself create curiosity — but a slow zoom with no story payoff "feels empty" [HHS-FORBES]. Schoonmaker and Scorsese will hold a shot longer than is comfortable, then cut when it is justified, and will "slap the audience in the face if it's required." Invisible cutting is not the goal; shaping the experience is [SCHOON].
- **A pacing break without breaking the pace.** Hoyos will not say "let's get started" after a dense hook, because a third expository beat makes people forget the first two (recency). She replaces it with a line that already moves the story ("so I cooked illegally") [HOYOS-YT].
- **Leave organic air and human reaction.** Paddy notes Beast leaves unpolished moments in; Hayden uses leitmotifs and sound to heighten a moment rather than cutting faster through it; craft-standards already forbids stripping every pause in speech [PADDY-ISLAND][HHS-FORBES].
- **One idea at a time.** Working memory is limited; piling new claims, graphics and jokes into the same second overloads it and people bail even if the frame is "busy enough" [SWELL]. TikTok's "one key message at a time" is the platform version of the same point (already in craft-standards).

**Must:** never cut on a fixed interval as a style rule. **Must:** a held shot has a reason (information being absorbed, a face reacting, tension, a "wow" the viewer needs to take in). **Should:** the opening of a short or a YouTube cold open is visually dense; the film then *varies* speed — acceleration into a peak, release on a hold — rather than staying at intro-speed for the whole runtime. **Should:** if nothing in the picture, sound or information has changed for long enough that a first-time viewer could look away, change something that serves the story.

**Freedom:** the rate, the device, and the look of every change.

## 1.7 Faces, eye contact, motion and contrast

**Why.** Human faces, especially direct gaze, capture and hold visuospatial attention more than most objects [GAZE][FACES]. Motion and contrast trigger orienting. That is why a face looking at the lens, a sudden move, or a bright subject on a dark field will stop a scroll faster than a pretty landscape.

- Hayden: "The story is revealing that, and making a response" — when Logan says he jumped off the top rope, the edit shows the jump [HHS-FORBES]. Show the person doing the thing, not a decorated talking head describing it later.
- Google's ABCD (already in craft-standards): if the film has people, open on them, ideally speaking to the viewer; tight framing; more than two shots in the first 5 s for *ads*. For documentaries, a held "wow" face or scale shot can be the first hook, then the cutting starts [ABCD][MB-MEMO][PADDY-ISLAND].
- Paddy, on Beast thumbnails (the same logic applies to the first frame): eyes get brightened because "the eyes are the first thing we focus on when we see human faces" [PADDY-ISLAND].
- Johnny Harris starts from a visual a stranger can feel before they are briefed: "look at this thing," then the context [HARRIS-VIS][HARRIS-JY].
- Contrast and scale are retention tools, not only design tools. A zoom-out that reveals the size of a set, a hard cut from a close face to a wide spectacle, a colour that pops against the last shot — these reset attention because they are new, not because they are "on brand" [PADDY-ISLAND][SOK].

**Must:** in any film that has a person, a face is on screen in the opening hook unless the customer has asked for a faceless film, or the hook *is* a non-human visual anchor (a document, a crash, a map, an object) that is more surprising than the face. Eyes and mouth stay in the safe box per `craft-standards.md`. **Should:** cut to the reaction, not away from it, on lines that need to be felt (this is also Murch: do not give up emotion, already in craft-standards §1.1).

## 1.8 Sound design and music shifts

**Why.** A change in the soundtrack is an attention reset that does not have to be a picture cut. Silence, after noise, makes people lean in. A new musical cue tells them the story just changed.

- Hayden uses *leitmotifs*: a short musical cue that, once learned, tells the viewer what kind of moment is coming (a fight cue, a driving cue). Sound effects are added to "heighten reality," not to decorate it [HHS-FORBES].
- Schoonmaker, on the opening of *Silence*: they stripped sound off the production logos, let cicadas build, then cut them "suddenly as the title 'SILENCE' appears on the screen – with no sound at all – dead silence," so the audience can "calm down… and start to engage." A beat of quiet before a reveal is a pattern interrupt made of absence [SCHOON-SIL].
- Paddy's island-video open mixes music, a hard stop, and a riser under a tease montage — audio changes riding the picture changes [PADDY-ISLAND]. That is analysis of one open, not a mix recipe to copy.
- MrBeast: "You can't fake intensity in videos." If the sound is hyped and the picture is not, people feel the lie [MB-MEMO].
- Ducking, loudness and "dialogue first" stay in `craft-standards.md` §1.12. This file only adds the *structural* job: use a music or silence change at the same moment the story re-hooks, and never let a brand-read or a generic bed signal "the good part stopped."

**Must:** music may not fight speech (craft rule). **Should:** plan at least one deliberate music or silence shift at a story turn, and a distinct end-of-film button (a hit, a hold, or a cut to quiet) rather than letting the bed fade out under a goodbye. **Freedom:** score, motifs, and the sound world of the film.

## 1.9 Captions and on-screen text as a retention tool

**Why.** On phones, many people watch with the sound off, or in a noisy room. Text is a second hook channel and a progress marker. It is not decoration, and it is not a substitute for a picture that is already doing the job.

- Hoyos: the hook must be understandable with the sound off [HOYOS-CS]. A short line of on-screen text that states the promise ("$10 in Miami," "I cooked it for $1") is doing hook work.
- Her mechanism graphics (the running $10 budget, "3 steps") keep the circle visibly closing. That is text used as a *retention clock*, not as a subtitle [HOYOS-MFM].
- Typography, timing, contrast, line length, safe zones, and "never cover the mouth" remain exactly as in `craft-standards.md` §1.9–1.10. If text cannot be read in the safe box at BBC/Netflix rates, it cannot retain anyone.
- One message at a time. A hook title plus captions plus a lower third plus a big number, all new, is cognitive load, not clarity [SWELL].

**Must:** on vertical social and podcast clips, the hook line is visible as well as audible in the first 3 s, unless the picture alone is unmistakable. **Must:** all text follows craft-standards for safe zone, contrast and duration. **Should:** if the film has a mechanism (a countdown, a list, a score, a budget), that state lives on screen and updates when the story updates. **Freedom:** type, motion, colour, and whether the hook title is a sentence, a fragment, or a number.

## 1.10 Story structure: stakes, tension, escalation, payoff

**Why.** People stay for an unfinished question whose answer they care about, and they judge the whole experience by its most intense moment and its ending (the peak-end rule), not by the average of every second [PEAK]. A film that is "pretty all the way through" and then shrugs off loses to a film with a real peak and a clean landing.

- Fredrickson and Kahneman (1993): retrospective ratings of an episode depend on snapshots of the most intense moment and the ending, and largely neglect duration [PEAK]. That is why a strong ending can redeem a long film, why an abrupt, satisfying last beat outperforms a long goodbye, and why Hoyos and MrBeast both end *immediately* after the payoff [HOYOS-CS][MB-MEMO]. It is also why Paddy describes Beast videos as finishing on a "high point," dramatic or wholesome, "what leaves the lasting impression" [PADDY-ISLAND].
- Hillier-Smith's three demands on every stretch of the edit: **emotion** (how to feel), **story** (what is happening), **rhythm** (what is coming next). Hitting two of the three is not enough [HHS-FORBES]. This sits cleanly next to Murch's Rule of Six in craft-standards §1.1 (emotion first, then story, then rhythm): we are not replacing Murch, we are using a YouTube editor who arrived at the same order.
- Stakes have to be personal or concrete, not abstract. Hoyos: a "cook for strangers" video is baseline until "my kitchen is broken, and I want to raise money to fix it by cooking" — a personal why, plus irony [HOYOS-CS]. Harris picks questions a viewer might be embarrassed to ask, and will not green-light a story that has no visual way to *show* the tension [HARRIS-JY].
- Escalate; do not add side quests. Stair-stepping, narrowing fields, rising budgets, and "but / then" turns are all the same move: the next beat costs more than the last one [MB-MEMO][HOYOS-MKT].
- "No dull moments" and "you can't fake intensity" [MB-MEMO]. If a 20-second stretch could be removed without anyone missing a fact, a feeling, or a loop, remove it. Eves would "kill the baby" even if the shot is beautiful [EVES-NS].

**Must:** the film has a stake the audience can say in one sentence, a visible escalation or complication, and a payoff that matches the hook. **Should:** the peak (the most intense or surprising beat) and the ending are designed on purpose, not leftover. **Freedom:** the story shape (survival, investigation, list with a twist, argument, portrait) — invent it for this film. Do not drop films into a fixed five-act template.

## 1.11 Endings: no goodbye drop-off, loops, end screens

**Why.** The ending is half of how the experience will be remembered [PEAK]. The moment you signal "we're done," a slice of the remaining audience leaves, and you lose both the last seconds of watch time and any end-screen click.

- **Abrupt endings.** MrBeast memo, twice: "have a satisfying payoff at the end of the video with an abrupt ending," and in the working rules, "The video endings must always be abrupt to protect retention" [MB-MEMO]. Hoyos, independently, on Shorts: "Whatever you say you're gonna do, you end it right after you do it" [HOYOS-CS]. No "thanks for watching," no recap that restates the answer they just got, no lingering logo.
- **Do not signal the end early**, except to hype the payoff [MB-MEMO].
- **Short-form loops.** A loop (last frame cutting cleanly back to the first, or a backtrack structure that starts and ends on the same strong moment) invites a rewatch. Hoyos treats rewatches as the difference between a Short that sits under 100% and one that takes off, especially under 30 s [HOYOS-YT][HOYOS-CS]. Twists and tight how-tos are rewatch engines because people want to re-see the tell, or to catch a step they missed [HOYOS-MFM]. YouTube Shorts "engaged views" **do not count loops** [YT-AN] — already noted in craft-standards. Still use a loop when it is honest; just do not mistake looped plays for engaged views.
- **Long-form end screens.** YouTube end screens may occupy only the **last 5–20 seconds**, and the video must be at least 25 s long [YT-END]. Put them *after* (or tight over the tail of) the payoff, never instead of it. The Beast/Eves attitude toward the next-video ask is: if you have earned a forward hook ("the next thing you will want to know is…"), use it; do not replace the ending with a subscribe lecture.

**Must:** the film ends on the payoff, or on the human reaction to the payoff, then stops. **Must:** no goodbye speech, no post-payoff logo sting that exists only to sign off. **Should:** shorts are loop-friendly when the first and last frames belong to each other. **Should:** long-form end screens, if used, live in the last 5–20 s and ride a forward question, not a recap.

## 1.12 Where retention tactics hurt quality

These are limits, not optional taste. Crossing them can raise a 10-second graph and still fail the film, the customer, or the next click.

1. **Clickbait that the video does not pay off.** Packaging mismatch is the official YouTube intro failure [YT-RET] and the memo's bouncy-castle story [MB-MEMO]. It also trains the audience to distrust the next film. Extreme is fine; a lie is not [MB-MEMO].
2. **Paying the title off too early**, then coasting. The graph may look lively; Preece's "gradual realization" still kills it [PREECE].
3. **Withholding the payoff forever**, or skipping it on a short to "keep them watching." Hoyos: close the circle [HOYOS-CS][HOYOS-MFM].
4. **Metronome cutting and constant stimulation.** Schoonmaker: a shot has its own life [SCHOON]. Paddy: it is not "lots of cuts and talking really fast" [PADDY-ISLAND]. Overstimulation is its own monotony, and it flattens the peak the ending needs.
5. **Cute or expensive shots that break the journey.** Eves: kill them [EVES-NS]. Craft-standards already says substance first (Dmytryk).
6. **Fake intensity** — hype sound, hype captions, nothing actually happening [MB-MEMO].
7. **Signalled interruptions** (scripted mid-rolls, "welcome to the channel," chapter cards that reset energy to zero). Beast's old brand-deal graphs show a *crater*: people skipped the read and some clicked off. When the same slot was treated as entertainment, the dip was "way less dramatic" [MB-MEMO].
8. **Optimising only the graph.** Hoyos's 70% Short that still hit 10 million, and her friends' 100% Shorts that did not, are the warning [HOYOS-CS]. Do not cut the human moment, the shareable surprise, or a true complication to smooth a dip.
9. **Over-claiming the science.** Do not write "Zeigarnik says" or "every 3 seconds the brain resets" in a treatment. Use Loewenstein, peak-end, orienting/habituation, faces, and cognitive load, and use them modestly [LOEW][PEAK][SOK][FACES][GAZE][SWELL][ZEIG-META].


# PART 2. Standards by video type

Platform hook timings already in `craft-standards.md` §1.15 still apply. What follows is the structural layer on top.

## 2.1 Short vertical social films (15–60 s)

The 30- and 60-second social films (a portrait, a cold-open documentary bite, a vertical explainer). Designed for TikTok, Reels and Shorts, where the first frame is the only packaging you may get.

**Must**
- First frame is a hook on its own, readable with the sound off [HOYOS-CS].
- Verbal and visual promise (what this is, why stay) lands in the **first 1–3 s**. Instagram's View Rate is measured at 3 s [IG-HOOK]; TikTok's ad guidance still wants the proposition in 3 s and the hook by 5–6 s (`craft-standards.md` §1.15).
- The script foreshadows the ending in or right after the hook, then pays it off on screen, then **ends immediately** [HOYOS-CS][HOYOS-MFM][HOYOS-YT].
- Progress is visible (a mechanism, a list of steps, a "but / then," a narrowing question). Viewers must be able to feel the circle closing [HOYOS-MFM].
- No logo-first intro, no "welcome back," no goodbye [EVES-5][MB-MEMO][HOYOS-CS].
- The hook is a claim the film can honestly finish. No bait-and-switch [MB-MEMO][YT-RET].

**Should**
- Prefer action and power in the first words, not "This is…" [HOYOS-MFM].
- After a dense hook, the next line should move the story, not announce that the story is starting [HOYOS-YT].
- A mid-point re-hook or "but / then" around the halfway mark [HOYOS-MKT].
- End frame is loop-friendly when it does not strain the story [HOYOS-CS][NBCU].
- For films **under 30 s**, design a reason to rewatch (a twist, a tight how-to, a loop) — Hoyos's observation, not a platform quota [HOYOS-YT].
- On-screen hook text plus a living mechanism graphic, inside the safe box, at craft-standards reading rates.

**Freedom**
- The hook device, the mechanism's design, whether to loop, the entire look, and whether the film is a portrait, a process, a joke with a turn, or a 30-second documentary.

## 2.2 Documentary, biography and explainer (about 2–10 minutes, and the same architecture up to ~15)

This is the Johnny Harris / Vox-shaped film and the short documentary or explainer, including Beast-length films when a job runs that long. The memo's 0–1 / 1–3 / 3–6 / back-half marks were measured on ~13-minute videos; scale them to the actual runtime rather than copying the clock.

**Must**
- Opening **30 s** validate the title/thumbnail or the stated brief, with no channel-logo intro [YT-RET][EVES-NS][MB-MEMO].
- A main open loop is planted in the open (a question, a stake, a "look at this") and not fully paid off until late [HARRIS-JY][LOEW].
- After the hook, *show* rather than recap: visual anchor first, context second [HARRIS-JY][HARRIS-VIS][MB-MEMO].
- At least one mid-film re-engagement (new stake, new question, or a story-fit spectacle) before the halfway mark, and another before the payoff on films longer than ~6 minutes [MB-MEMO]. On a 3-minute film, one mid-point re-hook is enough.
- The most important content is not hoarded for the end; a piece of it appears early enough that the first half can win them [YT-RET][MB-MEMO].
- Do not answer the title in minute one and then review the leftovers [PREECE].
- Abrupt ending on the payoff; no "thanks for watching" [MB-MEMO]. End screens, if any, only in the last 5–20 s [YT-END].
- Writing stays concrete and clear. Harris aims at roughly a fifth-to-eighth-grade reading level so a stranger can follow [HARRIS-JY]. That is a clarity target, not a vocabulary ban.

**Should**
- First 5–15 s: a visual hook or question, not an establishing tour [YT-FOUR][HHS-FORBES][HARRIS-VIS].
- Minutes 1–3 (or the first quarter, on a shorter film): crazy progression — compress setup so the viewer is already invested [MB-MEMO].
- Question-to-report cycles: each answer opens a tighter question [HARRIS-JY].
- Two-column thinking even if the engine is not "script vs picture" on paper: every narration line has a planned visual that carries meaning, not B-roll wallpaper [HARRIS-JY].
- Establish scale or stakes with a real image (a zoom-out, a document, a number the viewer can see) [PADDY-ISLAND][HARRIS-VIS].
- Hold shots that need to be felt; cut faster through connective tissue [HHS-FORBES][SCHOON].
- A distinct high point near the end (peak-end) [PADDY-ISLAND][PEAK].
- If a sponsorship or chapter card must appear, treat it as content, not a commercial break [MB-MEMO].

**Freedom**
- The investigative device, the world, the typography, the camera language, whether the film is a single-question explainer or a multi-scene portrait. No prescribed act template beyond "hook, invest, re-hook, pay off, stop."

## 3.2 Edit-stage checklist (against the cut, before delivery)

- [ ] **First frame.** Would this stop you mid-scroll with the sound off? Is a face or a striking visual anchor in it? [HOYOS-CS][HARRIS-VIS].
- [ ] **First 1–3 s (short) / first 5–15 s then 30 s (long).** Promise confirmed, not described. YouTube long-form: would this still be plausibly above a 50% hold at 30 s? [YT-RET][EVES-5][IG-HOOK].
- [ ] **Visual-change cadence.** The open is dense enough that a stranger does not get a static first second. After that, changes (cuts, moves, text, sound, new information) follow the *story*, not a clock. No stretch where picture, sound and information all sit still long enough to look away, unless that hold *is* the tension [PADDY-ISLAND][SCHOON][HHS-FORBES].
- [ ] **Dead air.** Connecting tissue is tight. Pauses that land a line, and reactions, are intact. No robotic gap-strip, no empty pad to hit a runtime.
- [ ] **Drop-off inspection.** Watch once as a stranger and mark any moment you would leave. Pay special attention to: the hook, the first-minute handoff, the mid-point, the pre-payoff build, any mid-roll or chapter card, and any line that sounds like an ending [YT-RET][MB-MEMO].
- [ ] **Loop still open?** At the mid-point, is there still something the viewer does not know and still wants? [LOEW][HOYOS-MFM].
- [ ] **Peak and end.** There is a designed high point, and the film stops on the payoff (or its reaction). No post-payoff slop [PEAK][MB-MEMO][HOYOS-CS].
- [ ] **Text.** Hook line and any mechanism are readable in the safe box; captions still follow craft-standards; one new message at a time [SWELL].
- [ ] **Sound.** A planned music or silence shift at a story turn. No fake intensity. Dialogue still wins the mix (craft §1.12) [HHS-FORBES][MB-MEMO].
- [ ] **Trust.** The cut does not invent a claim, retime a reaction onto the wrong line, or promise a payoff it does not show.
